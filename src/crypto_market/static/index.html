Does a burst of large transfers predict price?

Signal: average on-chain transaction size (est. transfer volume ÷ transaction count, from blockchain.info) vs. its trailing 90-day robust average. A day where that ratio spikes is a day with disproportionately large transfers — a free proxy for "whale" activity, without exchange address labels. Rule: buy at the close of a spike day, sell N days later; legs never overlap.

Does "day trading" work at hourly resolution?

Hourly BTC bars from Bitfinex (public, back to 2013) — the finest free history available, and the actual timescale "day trading" implies. Two tests: is any hour of day (UTC) reliably better or worse, and does the direction of the last N hours predict the next N (momentum), or invert (reversal)? Every position flip costs slippage, since these rules can flip thousands of times.

Is the same coin ever cheaper on one exchange than another — for long enough to catch?

The one fast, small edge that is actually well-defined: buy BTC where it's cheap, sell it where it's dear, at the same moment. Two datasets, two questions. Thirty days of 1-minute bars from three venues (public) say whether a gap exists and whether a "see it this minute, trade it the next" rule makes money after fees. Live best-bid/best-ask ticks, recorded by this server from each venue's public feed since it last started, say how many milliseconds a gap lasts — the number that decides whether a home connection could ever reach it.

1 bp = 0.01%. Retail fee tiers for a market order: Coinbase ≈ 60 bp, Bitstamp ≈ 40, Kraken ≈ 40, Bitfinex ≈ 20. Every round trip here pays the fee four times (buy and sell on both venues, then unwind both).

Live ticks: how long does an executable gap last?

A moment is "executable" when selling on one venue and buying on the other beats both fees, right now. An episode is an unbroken run of such moments. "Open after latency" is the share of episodes still there when your order could arrive. Rows marked steady are a venue's persistent premium: executable almost continuously, but only once — after that round you hold the wrong asset on each side.
