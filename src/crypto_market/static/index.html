Triangles: do an exchange's own three prices ever disagree — for long enough to catch?

Every coin an exchange lists both in dollars and in a bridge currency (BTC, ETH, USDT, on Binance.US and Kraken also USDC, and on Kraken also the euro, which it lists against the dollar) is a triangle: its three prices should agree (X/USD == X/Q × Q/USD). When they don't, three trades in a loop — dollars → coin → bridge → dollars, or the reverse — end with more dollars than they started with, before fees. Nothing leaves the exchange and no second account is involved; the cost is three fees and whatever has moved by the time the orders land. This server watches every one of those markets on the exchange's own feed — Coinbase's full order books, Binance.US's and Kraken's best bid and ask — and re-prices both loops of every triangle each time one of its three markets changes, so the numbers below are what a bot sitting on the same feed would have seen, down to the millisecond. "Beat fees" counts episodes where what was left after your latency exceeded the loop's three fees, and "$ / day" is what taking every one of those, on the size available at the peak, would have paid. Resting orders on Binance.US: twenty strategies replayed against a month of real trades

Binance.US charges nothing for an order that rests on the book and is filled by someone else's trade (2 bp when you fill someone else's). A resting order only fills when somebody trades at your price, so the honest test is to replay every trade that actually happened and ask, for each one, whether it would have filled an order you had sitting there. Two fill rules bracket the truth: through — a trade printed at a price beyond yours, so your whole price level was eaten and you were filled whatever your place in the queue (the pessimistic rule and the one to believe); touch — a trade at exactly your price fills you up to its size, as if you were first in line (optimistic). Orders are $100, the coin held may drift $500 either way from where it started, and an order takes 200 ms to arrive. Profit counts what is held at the end at the current price.

"Fills" is how many resting orders were filled; "immediate" is fills you paid 2 bp for (closing a position at once, or the other two sides of the triangle). "Held" is the share of the month with a position open. "Drawdown" is the largest drop from the best point so far. Click a row to see its profit over the month under both rules.

Everything else: the same coin across exchanges, and the BTC/USDT/USD triangle on other venues

Is the same coin ever cheaper on one exchange than another — for long enough to catch?

The one fast, small edge that is actually well-defined: buy a coin where it's cheap, sell it where it's dear, at the same moment. BTC is the obvious market; the stablecoins (USDT, USDC, priced in dollars and in each other) are the interesting one — their price barely moves, so what's left is purely the venues disagreeing with each other, and several venues charge far less on these pairs. Two datasets, two questions. Thirty days of 1-minute bars (public) say whether a gap exists and whether a "see it this minute, trade it the next" rule makes money after fees. Live best-bid/best-ask ticks, recorded by this server from each venue's public feed since it last started, say how many milliseconds a gap lasts — the number that decides whether a home connection could ever reach it.

Live ticks: how long does an executable gap last?

A moment is "executable" when selling on one venue and buying on the other beats both fees, right now. An episode is an unbroken run of such moments. "Open after latency" is the share of episodes still there when your order could arrive. "Size at best price" is the median dollars actually on offer at the two prices (the smaller of the two order books' top levels), and "$ per episode" is the whole edge applied to that size — what one perfect, instant execution would have paid. Rows marked steady are a venue's persistent premium: executable almost continuously, but only once — after that round you hold the wrong asset on each side. "Uniswap on Base" is not an exchange but an on-chain pool, read once a block (≈ 2 s): it has one price, and trading with it costs its 0.05% fee each way, so that is shown as its spread; its size is the dollars you can trade before moving its price by 1 bp. An on-chain trade also pays under a cent of gas and lands a block later, and the coins live in a wallet, not an exchange account.

Triangle: are the three prices on one exchange consistent with each other?

Dollars → BTC → USDT → dollars (or the reverse), all on one venue, all at once. Nothing ever leaves the exchange, so there is no stranded balance to unwind and no second connection — but it is three fills, so the cost is three fees, and BTC/USDT is a thin market on the US venues (Coinbase's spread on it runs ≈ 4 bp against 0.1 bp on BTC/USD). The check is whether BTC/USD == BTC/USDT × USDT/USD; the mismatch, in basis points, is the gross profit of one round trip.

Fee per fill on BTC/USDT and USDT/USD is usually the venue's normal rate for the BTC side and its stablecoin rate for the USDT side; a single number is used here for simplicity. Kraken has no 30-day minute history (live only).
